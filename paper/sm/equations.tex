
\section{Detailed general methods with equations}

This section describes the general methods applied to all materials in more detail. The stock extrapolation (Subsection \ref{sec:stock_extrapol}) and the trade extrapolation (Subsection \ref{sec:trade_extrapol}) extend the corresponding methods sections of the main paper, while the trade adjustments (Subsection \ref{sec:trade_adjustments}) are additional information not covered in the main paper.


%=======================================================================
%=======================================================================
%=======================================================================
\subsection{Stock extrapolation} \label{sec:stock_extrapol}

In-use stock per capita for each region and end-use category is regressed on the logarithm of GDP per capita using a logistic function.

To fix conventions, let $L(p,x)$ be the logistic curve over $x$ parametrized by $p = (\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$, that is
\begin{equation}
    L(p,x) =  \alpha / (1 + \exp(-4\,\gamma\,(x - \beta))),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the saturation level, $\beta$ is the horizontal offset and $\gamma$ is the growth rate.
We define the normalized logistic function $\tilde{L}(\varphi,x) = L(p,x)/\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{L}(\varphi,x) =  1 / (1 + \exp(-4\,\gamma\,(x - \beta))),
\end{equation}
with reduced parameters $\varphi = (\beta,\,\gamma)$.

In the following, $t$ denotes the time variable (year), $r$ indexes the region, and $u$ indexes the in-use category.

We multiply the prescribed saturation level $\alpha_\text{tot}$ for each material with lifetime-weighted prescribed limit end-use inflow shares $\sigma_u$ to obtain the saturation level $\alpha_u$ for each in-use category $u$, i.e., $\alpha_u = \alpha_\text{tot} \, \sigma_u$.
The per-capita historical stocks $h_{r,u}(t)$ for each region and good (as calculated from the historical MFA) are normalized by this end-use-specific saturation level $\alpha_u$ to obtain the normalized historical stocks $\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t) = h_{r,u}(t)/\alpha_u$.

%=======================================================================
\subsubsection{Step 1: Common regression}

We first calculate the common regression for normalized per-capita in-use stocks $f^{(1)}_u(t)$ (where the superscript $(1)$ indicates Step 1 of the stock extrapolation procedure) using
\begin{equation}
f^{(1)}_u(t) = \tilde{L}\big(\hat{\varphi}^{(1)}_u,\,x_r(t)\big),
\end{equation}
where $x_r(t)$ is the base-10 logarithm of the GDP per capita\footnote{Strictly speaking, the GDP per capita is divided by 1 USD/cap to make it unitless before taking the logarithm.} of that region and
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\varphi}^{(1)}_u = \arg\min_{\varphi} \left( \sum_{t,r} (\tilde{L}(\varphi,x_r(t))-\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t))^2 \right)
\end{equation}
are the reduced parameters minimizing the $L^2$-distance from the historical stocks.


%=======================================================================
\subsubsection{Step 2: Regional adaptation}

In this step, we vary the parameter set from Step 1 by region. This step aims to balance continuation of historical in-use-stock development trends with limited deviation from the common regression in Step 1.

To this end, we define the regional curve
\begin{equation}
f^{(2)}_{r,u}(t) = L\big(\hat{p}^{(2)}_{r,u},\,x_r(t)\big)
\end{equation}
with the region-specific parameter set $\hat{p}^{(2)}_{r,u}$. Note that we allow deviating saturation levels $\hat{\alpha}^{(2)}_{r,u} \neq 1$, so we now use the full logistic function $L$ with parameter vector $p$. We obtain $\hat{p}^{(2)}_{r,u}$ from the minimization
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}^{(2)}_{r,u} = \arg\min_{p} \Big( W_{T_a}\,T_a(p) + W_{T_r}\,T_r(p) + W_{T_d}\,T_d(p) + W_{D_\alpha}\,D_\alpha(p) + W_{D_\beta}\,D_\beta(p) + W_{D_\gamma}\,D_\gamma(p) \Big)
\end{equation}
where $T_a$, $T_r$, $T_d$, $D_\alpha$, $D_\beta$, and $D_\gamma$ are penalty functions, and $W_i$ the corresponding weights.

The terms
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    T_a(p) &= \big(L(p,x_r(t_{0}))-\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t_{0}) \big)^2, \\
    T_r(p) &= \left(\frac{L(p,x_r(t_{0}))-\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t_{0})}{\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t_{0})} \right)^2, \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{and} \\
    T_d(p) &= \Big(\frac{d}{d t}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}\big(L(p,x_r(t))\big)-\frac{d}{d t}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}\big(\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t)\big) \Big)^2 \\
\end{split}
\end{equation}
all penalize deviations of the regionally adapted function from the normalized historical in-use stocks $\tilde{h}_{r,u}$ at the last historical time step $t_{0}$.
In particular, the terms $T_a$ and $T_r$ penalize absolute and relative deviations of the function value, whereas $T_d$ penalizes deviations of the derivative w.r.t. time\footnote{The derivative is taken with respect to time, not with respect to GDP per capita, to avoid numerical issues in case of stagnating or declining historical GDP per capita.}.

On the other hand, the terms
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    D_\alpha(p) &= (\alpha-1)^2, \\
    D_\beta(p) &= (\beta-\hat{\beta}^{(1)}_{u})^2, \quad\text{and} \\
    D_\gamma(p) &= (\gamma-\hat{\gamma}^{(1)}_{u})^2
\end{split}
\end{equation}
penalize deviations of the parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ from the parameters of the common regression $\hat{\varphi}^{(1)}_u = \bigl(\hat{\beta}^{(1)}_{u}, \hat{\gamma}^{(1)}_{u}\bigr)$.

The weights of these parameter terms are chosen empirically to achieve a good trade-off and are the same for all materials. Their values are
\begin{equation}
W_{T_a} = 20, \quad
W_{T_r} = 1, \quad
W_{T_d} = 4000\, \text{yr}^{-2}, \quad
W_{D_\alpha} = 10, \quad
W_{D_\beta} = 7.5, \quad \text{and} \quad
W_{D_\gamma} = 0.025.
\end{equation}

%=======================================================================
\subsubsection{Step 3: Transition smoothing} \label{sec:smoothing}

Since Step 2 does not guarantee an exact match of value and derivative to historical data, a blending procedure from historical data $\tilde{h}_{r,u}(t)$ to the logistic curve $f^{(2)}_{r,u}(t)$ is performed.

The same procedure is later also applied to in-use stock inflows (Section \ref{sec:inflow_smoothing}) and to trade (Section \ref{sec:trade_smoothing}). It is therefore described in a generic notation, applied separately to each region and category: $h(t)$ denotes the historical data up to the last historical year $t_{0}$, the target value is labeled $s(t)$ ($s$ as in "set point", another term for target in this context), and the smoothed transition is labeled $y(t)$. One or two dots denote first and second time derivatives $\dot{y}(t) = \frac{dy}{d t}$ and $\ddot{y}(t) = \frac{d^2y}{d t^2}$.

For the stocks, this notation means that $h(t) = \tilde{h}_{r,u}(t)$, $s(t) = f^{(2)}_{r,u}(t)$, and $y(t) = f^{(3)}_{r,u}(t)$.

The blending procedure is carried out in the time domain. The smoothed function $y(t)$ is obtained by solving an initial value problem (a differential equation) with initial conditions $y(t_{0}) = h(t_{0})$ and $\dot{y}(t_{0}) = v_0$, where the initial slope $v_0$ is estimated from the recent historical trend. Its second derivative is controlled as described below, and its function value and first derivative are determined by time integration.

The second derivative is determined to drive the smoothed function $y(t)$ towards its target $s(t)$. To this end, a critically damped PD-controller is used, which means that the second derivative has two terms: one is proportional to the difference in the function value (the "P" in "PD", as in "proportional"), whereas the other is proportional to the differences in the derivative (the "D" in "PD", as in "difference").

The resulting differential equation then reads
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pd_acc}
    \ddot{y}(t) = - 2k\; (\dot{y}(t) - \dot{s}(t^*)) - k^2\;(y(t)-s(t)).
\end{equation}
The constant $k$ is a damping factor which determines the convergence speed. It is set such that in a static problem with $\dot{s}(t)=0$ and $\dot{y}(t_{0})=0$, the difference $y(t)-s(t)$ is reduced by 95 \% within a pre-defined approaching time $\tau$, which is chosen between 50 and 75 years. The asterisk $t^*$ is part of a look-ahead modification described below.

The differential equation is solved with an explicit Euler scheme, that is by starting at $t=t_{0}$ and executing the following three assignments year by year.
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \ddot{y}(t) &= - 2k\; (\dot{y}(t) - \dot{s}(t^*)) - k^2\;(y(t)-s(t)) \\
    \dot{y}(t+\Delta t) &= \dot{y}(t) + \ddot{y}(t) \; \Delta t \\
    y_p(t+\Delta t) &= y(t) + \dot{y}(t+\Delta t) \; \Delta t \\
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here, $\Delta t$ is the time step width, one year in this case. The value $y_p(t)$ is the preliminary smoothed function. The actual smoothed function $y(t)$ is finally obtained through the nudging procedure
\begin{equation}
    y(t+\Delta t) = \theta(t+\Delta t)\, s(t+\Delta t) + (1-\theta(t+\Delta t))\, y_p(t+\Delta t)
\end{equation}
with the nudging factor
\begin{equation}
    \theta(t) = 1 - \exp\big(-\rho(t)\,\Delta t\big), \qquad
    \rho(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\tau} \left(\frac{t-t_{0}}{\tau}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The nudging rate $\rho(t)$ increases over time to accelerate the complete convergence of the smoothed function to the target function. The coefficient $\lambda$ is chosen such that the nudging alone would reduce the difference $y(t)-s(t)$ by 95 \% within $\tau$. In control theory, an integration term is often added to the PD-controller to achieve complete convergence, making it a PID controller. In the present case, this led to oscillations in the smoothed function, which is why a nudging procedure was used instead.

Since the set point $s(t)$ is known a priori for all years, its future development over time can be used by the algorithm. In particular, the derivative $\dot{s}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:pd_acc} is evaluated at a later time than $t$, i.e. at $t^*=t+c(t)$, where the look-ahead $c(t)$ decreases linearly from five years to zero within $\tau/2$.
This lessens overshoots of the smoothed function if the target function nears saturation.

\subsubsection{Denormalization}

Finally, the smoothed normalized per-capita stocks $f^{(3)}_{r,u}(t)$ are multiplied with the good-specific saturation level $\alpha_u$ and with the population of the region $r$ to obtain the in-use stock extrapolation for each region and end-use category.

\subsubsection{In-use stock inflow smoothing} \label{sec:inflow_smoothing}

The in-use stock inflow is first computed from the extrapolated stock with a stock-driven dynamic stock model. As this can cause abrupt changes of the inflow at $t_{0}$, the transition smoothing of Section \ref{sec:smoothing} is applied to the inflow, with $h(t)$ the historical inflow, $s(t)$ the inflow from the stock-driven model, and $y(t)$ the smoothed inflow. Negative values of the smoothed inflow are set to zero, and the in-use stock is recomputed from the smoothed inflow with an inflow-driven dynamic stock model. The resulting stock therefore deviates slightly from the extrapolation of Section \ref{sec:stock_extrapol}.

\subsection{Trade extrapolation} \label{sec:trade_extrapol}

For trade extrapolation, a heuristic was developed that produces plausible future scenarios.
As a first step, trade flows of individual regions are extrapolated into the future separately. In a second step, imports and exports are corrected to achieve global market clearing.

In the following, an independent notation is used, such that symbols also occurring in Section \ref{sec:stock_extrapol} do not denote the same variables.

%=======================================================================
\subsubsection{Regional extrapolation}

Regionally, a trade market fulfills the mass balance
\begin{equation} \label{eq:trade_extrapol_massb}
    p_r(t) + i_r(t) = e_r(t) + d_r(t),
\end{equation}
where $p$ denotes local production, $i$ denotes imports, $e$ denotes exports, and $d$ denotes local demand. Here, $t$ is the year and $r$ is the region.

In the future MFA, the production value chain from material production to end-use is calculated backward, starting with end-use goods demand.
This means that first, local demand for end-use products is given, and imports, exports, and local production of end-use products have to be calculated together to fulfill Eq. \eqref{eq:trade_extrapol_massb}.
Similarly, after calculating local raw material demand from local production of end-use goods, this local demand is known for the raw material trade market as well, and imports, exports, and local production of raw materials have to be calculated together to fulfill Eq. \eqref{eq:trade_extrapol_massb}.
For scrap or waste markets, on the other hand, supply is known (from the outflow of the in-use stock and collection rates). So for this stage, the local "production" is known, and imports, exports, and local "demand" have to be calculated together.

\subsubsection{Basic scaling}

Let us first consider the demand-driven case. Since imports and domestically produced goods are often treated as substitutes, it is a natural choice to keep the import share constant, i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:import_scaling_basic}
    \frac{i_r(t)}{d_r(t)} = \frac{i_r(t_{0})}{d_r(t_{0})},
\end{equation}
where $t_{0}$ is the last historical time step. Similarly, the share of locally produced goods that is exported may stay approximately constant, which leads to the scaling
\begin{equation} \label{eq:export_scaling_basic}
    \frac{e_r(t)}{p_r(t)} =  \frac{e_r(t_{0})}{p_r(t_{0})}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Scaling modification for increasing demand / production}

An issue with this implementation is that regions with still very low GDP and therefore low domestic demand and production sometimes have high import and/or export shares, partly due to lacking domestic supply/demand and partly due to lacking data. We thus let the import and export shares of these regions converge toward the global average import/export shares in the last historical year if production or demand have very high relative increases.

This is achieved with the formula

\begin{equation} \label{eq:import_scaling_incr}
    i_r(t) = d_r(t) \left(\frac{i_r(t_{0})}{d_r(t_{0})}\right)^{(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\sum_{r'} i_{r'}(t_{0})}{\sum_{r'} d_{r'}(t_{0})}\right)^{\alpha} \quad\text{for}\quad d_r(t) \geq d_r(t_{0})
\end{equation}
with a blending factor $0\leq\alpha\leq1$, which is a function of relative demand change, i.e., $\alpha(\,d_r(t)/d_r(t_{0})\,)$. In particular, we choose \( \alpha \) to be the quintic spline
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\delta) = 6\, \delta^5 - 15 \, \delta^4 + 10 \, \delta^3
\end{equation}
with $\delta$ being the logarithm of the relative demand change
\begin{equation}
\delta = \log_{50}(d_r(t)/d_r(t_{0})).
\end{equation}

Similarly, exports are scaled as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:export_scaling_incr}
    e_r(t) = p_r(t) \left(\frac{e_r(t_{0})}{p_r(t_{0})}\right)^{(1-\alpha)} \left(\frac{\sum_{r'} e_{r'}(t_{0})}{\sum_{r'} p_{r'}(t_{0})}\right)^{\alpha} \quad\text{for}\quad d_r(t) \geq d_r(t_{0}).
\end{equation}
Note that $\alpha$ depends on demand, not production, for simplicity.

\subsubsection{Scaling modification for decreasing demand}

For decreasing demand, a different phenomenon occurs: such regions have standing production capacity, which lowers production costs, and often protectionist policies to safeguard these capacities. We therefore assume that exports stay constant in this case, i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:export_scaling_decr}
    e_r(t) = e_r(t_{0}) \quad\text{for}\quad d_r(t) \leq d_r(t_{0})
\end{equation}
while imports are still scaled proportionally with demand as in Eq. \eqref{eq:import_scaling_basic}, i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:import_scaling_decr}
    i_r(t) = i_r(t_{0}) \frac{d_r(t)}{d_r(t_{0})}  \quad\text{for}\quad d_r(t) \leq d_r(t_{0}).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Solving for exports and production}

Imports only depend on the known variable demand and can therefore be calculated directly. Exports, on the other hand, depend on production (cf. Eq. \eqref{eq:export_scaling_incr}), which in turn is determined from the mass balance in Eq. \eqref{eq:trade_extrapol_massb} and thus depends on exports. This equation system is solved in a fixed-point iteration loop. This loop is initialized with the assumption
\begin{equation}
     \frac{p_r(t)}{p_r(t_{0})} =\frac{d_r(t)}{d_r(t_{0})}.
\end{equation}

In every iteration, Eqs. \eqref{eq:export_scaling_incr} or \eqref{eq:export_scaling_decr} are solved for $e_r(t)$, after which Eq. \eqref{eq:trade_extrapol_massb} is solved for $p_r(t)$. This updated $p_r(t)$ is then used for the next iteration. Ten iterations are used without a convergence criterion, which yields sufficient accuracy.

\subsubsection{Scrap and waste trade}

Scrap and waste trade is calculated analogously to the other trade stages, with the difference that the roles of imports and exports as well as those of demand and production are flipped.

This means that exports are calculated first. They are still scaled with production, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:export_scaling_basic}. Imports are scaled with demand, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:import_scaling_basic}. Imports and demand are then calculated concurrently with a fixed-point iteration.
In case of increasing scrap/waste production, the Eqs. \eqref{eq:export_scaling_incr} and \eqref{eq:import_scaling_incr} are used, while for decreasing production, \eqref{eq:export_scaling_decr} and \eqref{eq:import_scaling_decr} are used.

\subsubsection{Transition smoothing} \label{sec:trade_smoothing}

The transition smoothing of Section \ref{sec:smoothing} is applied to the extrapolated imports $i_r(t)$ and exports $e_r(t)$ before global balancing. For each, $h(t)$ is the historical trade flow, $s(t)$ the extrapolated trade flow, and $y(t)$ the smoothed trade flow. Negative values of the smoothed trade flows are set to zero.

%=======================================================================
\subsubsection{Global balancing} \label{sec:trade_balancing}

Global market clearing prescribes that global exports equal global imports, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \sum_r e_r(t) = \sum_r i_r(t).
\end{equation}
This condition is not necessarily fulfilled after the extrapolation step. Therefore imports and/or exports are multiplied such that their global sums have the same common value $g(t)$ (defined below), i.e.
 \begin{equation}
    e_r(t) = \tilde{e}_r(t) \frac{g(t)}{\sum_{r'} (\tilde{e}_{r'}(t))},
    \qquad
    i_r(t) = \tilde{i}_r(t) \frac{g(t)}{\sum_{r'} (\tilde{i}_{r'}(t))}
\end{equation}
Here, the tilde accent denotes values before the scaling step, and the apostrophe is only added to the summing index $r'$ so as not to be confused with the equation index $r$.

For future trades, the common value $g(t)$ is determined as the geometric mean of imports and exports, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    g(t) = \sqrt{\sum_r (\tilde{e}_r(t)) \; \sum_r (\tilde{i}_r(t))}
    \quad\text{for}\quad t > t_{0}.
\end{equation}

The above balancing is also performed for historical trades, as exogenous import and export data from the literature do not always sum to the same global values. In this case, it is assumed that trade volumes are rather under-reported than over-reported, such that the maximum is used for the global common sum instead, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    g(t) = \max \left( \sum_r (\tilde{e}_r(t)),\; \sum_r (\tilde{i}_r(t))\right)
    \quad\text{for}\quad t \leq t_{0}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Trade adjustments} \label{sec:trade_adjustments}

Exogenous trade data are not always consistent with exogenous production and demand data, and extrapolated trade does not account for how much secondary material a region can use. Without correction, this can result in negative flows or unused secondary material. The following mechanisms prevent this.

\subsubsection{Trade capping}

In historical years, the net exports of a region are limited to its domestic supply, e.g., net steel exports to domestic steel production. This is applied to the steel and manufactured products trade of steel, the primary and manufactured products trade of plastics, and the cement trade. Gross exports may still exceed supply if they are covered by imports, which represents re-export trade. Net exports are evaluated per good category, since net imports of one category cannot be re-exported as another category. Where the limit is exceeded, exports are reduced accordingly.

Reducing the exports of some regions violates global market clearing. It is restored with the global balancing of Section \ref{sec:trade_balancing}, where only the opposite trade flows of the unaffected regions are scaled. As this may partially reintroduce the excess, capping and balancing are iterated until convergence.

Analogously, historical net imports are limited to domestic demand for clinker trade (cement) and primary plastics trade. For plastics, the excess net imports of a polymer are first reassigned to other polymers of the same polymer type, as far as their demand allows; only the remainder is removed.

The same procedure is applied to future steel scrap trade: net scrap imports of a region are limited to the amount that its scrap-based steel production can use in addition to domestic scrap. Scrap that is not imported remains in the exporting regions.

\subsubsection{Surplus redistribution}

In future years, some regions have more secondary material than they can use domestically, while others could use more. This surplus is redistributed with an auxiliary trade: regions export their surplus and import up to their remaining capacity, and both are scaled to the smaller of the two global sums. This applies to steel scrap exceeding the maximum scrap input to steel production, where the auxiliary trade is added to the extrapolated scrap trade. For plastics, it applies to mechanically recycled polymers and chemically recycled feedstock, each with its own auxiliary trade market.

\subsubsection{Trade-adjusted end-use shares}

Historical inflows into the in-use stock are disaggregated into end-use categories with exogenous end-use shares. This is applied to steel (by end use) and plastics (by end use and polymer). The inflow of each category consists of domestic manufacturing and net imports of manufactured goods. If the net imports of one category exceed the inflow given by its end-use share, domestic manufacturing of this category would become negative. In this case, the inflow of the category is set to its net imports instead. The resulting surplus is compensated by reducing the domestic manufacturing of the remaining categories proportionally. Total inflow is thereby conserved, and the end-use shares deviate from the exogenous values only where necessary.
